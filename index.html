Learn AI Red Team with Phoebe

Three thousand hours found nothing. That was not the same as nothing being there.

A bug bounty ran 183 participants over three thousand hours against a new safety system and found no universal jailbreak. A later public exercise with 339 people and a few hundred more hours found one. Nothing about the first result was wrong; it simply did not mean what "we red-teamed it and found nothing" is usually taken to mean. This is a course about testing AI systems you own, honestly, and about what you may claim afterwards. It covers scoping and rules of engagement, the attack classes named by OWASP 2026, MITRE ATLAS and NIST, the defences that hold and the measured evidence that most of them do not, how to score a finding when the same input gives a different answer every time, and how findings become a standing suite. At its centre, a live attack-surface board where an untested class counts at full risk rather than zero, because a coverage meter that treats "we did not look" as "we are fine" is worse than no meter at all. Every exercise runs against a fictional company, inside a scope document you write in the first session.

Authorized testing only, and where this sits Everything here is about systems you own or are contracted in writing to test. The course names attack classes so you can build test cases and pairs each with its defence; it contains no exploit payloads. Agent Launch has a safety gate that requires an adversarial slice and does not teach you to build one - that is this course. AI Observability owns the telemetry findings surface in. Judgement mode: what a red team can and cannot establish, the threat model in two heuristics, how to read a report without being fooled by a number, how to score severity by what a finding chains to, which defences the measured evidence actually supports, and when the right answer is that this workflow should not ship as an agent at all.

The threat model

Two heuristics do most of the work: the lethal trifecta, and the rule of two. Remove any one leg and the risk goes with it.

The map 🔬 Leader 3 · medium

Reading a red-team report

A near-zero attack success rate is now the most dangerous number in the field. Twelve defences reported one; most were broken above ninety percent.

The grilling kit ⚖️ Leader 4 · medium

Severity and triage

The same jailbreak is informational in one system and critical in another. Severity is what it chains to, not how clever the prompt was.

What it chains to 🛡️ Leader 5 · deciding

What defences actually hold

Every individual defence was beaten by a third to sixty percent of teams that tried. Stacking cut it to sixteen percent, not to zero.

The evidence 🛑 Leader 6 · deciding

When to stop

The regulator says it plainly: if the residual risk is intolerable, it may not be a good use case for an LLM. That is a finding.
